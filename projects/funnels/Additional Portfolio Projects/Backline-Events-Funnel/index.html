Every Event Has Failure Points.
Ours Are Written Down Before You Arrive.

Most coordinators sell you a timeline. A timeline only describes the day that goes right. Two weeks out we write a second document, the one nobody shows you, covering the day that doesn’t.

The Run Book

Sixty to ninety lines, printed twice, carried by two people. Every vendor’s arrival window, the name and mobile of the person actually driving the van, and the backup we call if that van doesn’t move.

It means a florist who cancels at 9 a.m. costs you a phone call you never hear about, instead of the centrepieces.

A team on site, not a number to call

Two coordinators minimum, three past 120 guests, on the floor from load-in to load-out. Nobody is running your day from a laptop across town, so a problem gets solved by someone standing in the room where it happened.

Problems solved behind you

Our rule is that you find out at brunch the next morning. We fix it, we log it, and we tell you afterwards, which means the version of the day you remember is the one you planned.

One walkthrough, on site, before we quote

We stand in your venue and find the failure points specific to it: the loading door that’s too narrow, the lawn that floods, the kitchen that can’t plate 140 covers at once. You get the list whether or not you hire us.

Your On-Site Command

Three roles, assigned by name at your walkthrough, on your run book, and in the room on the day. You will have met all of them before anyone arrives.

  1. Lead Coordinator

    Owns the clock and makes the calls.

    Holds the run book, decides when to compress the timeline, and is the only person who needs to talk to you. If a call has to be made in ninety seconds, they make it and tell you after.

    Dana Okafor
  2. Vendor Lead

    Owns every supplier, load-in to load-out.

    Confirms each vendor the week of, meets their van at the door, and carries the backup list. When someone is late, they are already on the phone to the second name before anyone inside notices.

    Marcus Webb
  3. Floor Captain

    Owns the room and the things nobody else sees.

    Watches guests rather than schedules: the grandmother who needs a chair, the over-served cousin, the toast that is running nine minutes long. Handles it quietly, away from the room.

    Talia Novak

Backup for Every Failure Point

These are the six things that actually go wrong, in order of how often we see them. Every one has an answer written down before your event date.

Common event failure points and the backup already in place for each
Failure point What it looks like on the day The backup already in place
A vendor doesn’t show The florist confirms Thursday, then stops answering at 9 a.m. Saturday. A second vendor in every category, called the week of and paid to hold the date. Swap decided by 10 a.m., not at the door.
The weather turns Rain arrives ninety minutes before an outdoor ceremony that has no cover. A tent on four-hour hold from March to October. The call gets made six hours out on the forecast, not on the first drop.
The timeline slips Speeches run twenty-five minutes long and dinner service backs up behind them. Three compression points marked in the run book. The kitchen gets the call before the plates do, and dinner still lands hot.
Power or sound dies The DJ rig cuts out four bars into the first dance. A battery speaker and a spare mic in our van at every event. Music back inside four minutes, from a playlist we already hold.
Someone gets hurt or ill A guest faints during the toasts, in a room with two hundred people watching. Two coordinators first-aid certified. Venue medical contact and nearest urgent care confirmed at the walkthrough, on the run book.
A guest becomes the problem An over-served guest, or the family conflict everyone was quietly dreading. The floor captain moves it out of the room, arranges a ride, and stays with it. You hear about it tomorrow, if at all.

Crises You Never Knew About

Three events where something went badly wrong. In all three, the couple or the client found out afterwards.

4:10 p.m. · 40 minutes to guest arrival

The cake was still in a broken-down van.

The bakery’s driver called from the shoulder of Highway 26. Our vendor lead had their second van dispatched inside six minutes and moved cake-cutting forty minutes later in the run book.

“I found out about the van at brunch the next day. At the time I thought the later cake-cutting was just how we had planned it.”

Elena R., wedding, Hood River

11:00 a.m. · 90 minutes to the ceremony

Rain, and a lawn ceremony with no cover.

The forecast turned at nine. We released the tent hold at eleven rather than waiting for rain, and the crew had it up by half past twelve. The ceremony started on schedule, dry, in front of 96 guests.

“My mother still thinks we planned the tent all along. Nobody asked me a single question that morning.”

Priya C., wedding, Sauvie Island

9:48 p.m. · four bars into the first dance

The DJ’s rig died in front of everyone.

A blown amp, mid-song. The battery speaker came out of our van and the track restarted from our own copy of the playlist. Three minutes and forty seconds of quiet, and most of the room read it as a pause in the song.

“Our photographer told us what happened a week later. We genuinely had not noticed.”

Owen M., wedding, Portland
